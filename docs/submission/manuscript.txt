Detecting post-acute COVID-19 vaccination syndrome in pharmacovigilance data: a pre-registered analysis of v-safe free text

Matthew Halma¹

¹ Open Source Medicine, Tallinn, Estonia. ORCID: 0000-0003-2487-0636. Corresponding author: Matthew Halma, Director, Open Source Medicine, Pärnu mnt. 139c, 11317 Tallinn, Estonia; mhalma@opensourcemed.info.

Running title: PACVS signature in v-safe free text. Article type: Original Research Article. Draft of 2026-11-12.

Abstract

Introduction. Post-acute COVID-19 vaccination syndrome (PACVS) is a chronic multi-system condition that pharmacovigilance systems code one symptom at a time. CDC's v-safe collected free-text answers at 3, 6 and 12 months after a dose; one prior analysis used its structured answers [22], none its free text.

Objective. To test, under a pre-registered protocol, whether long-term v-safe free text shows a coherent, persistent and under-captured multi-domain symptom pattern.

Methods. Four questions were registered (osf.io/gnfpd) before any long-term text was read: clustering of nine candidate symptom domains, early onset and persistence, under-capture and dispersed coding in VAERS, and burden. Text was classified by a frozen keyword lexicon plus a language model and validated against CDC's MedDRA coding, with a confirmatory gate of precision and recall of at least 0.85.

Results. Only fatigue passed the gate, so the primary analysis could not be run; every result is the registered nine-domain sensitivity analysis. Of 7,819,251 entries, 1,595,121 were long-term, and of 8.62 million person-doses with a long-term survey, 87,229 (1.01%) met the case definition. Domain pairs co-occurred 1.25 times as often as chance (p = 0.001) in every dose and manufacturer stratum and in 1.06 million VAERS reports. Nulls matched on how much each person wrote, or fixing each person-dose's own domain count, removed that aggregate excess but left a core in which cognitive symptoms co-occurred with sleep 2.3 to 2.7 times and with fatigue 2.1 to 2.3 times more than chance, in women and in men. The registered onset test was uninformative (28% early against a schedule-dependent 71% expected). Symptoms recurred at a later survey in 47 to 60% of cases, depending on how cases qualified, against 1.5 to 10.5% of comparison groups. Cases reached VAERS about as often as named acute diagnoses (rate ratio 0.94, 95% CI 0.90 to 0.98), with less dispersed coding.

Conclusion. 1.01% of person-doses with a long-term v-safe survey describe a multi-domain pattern whose cognitive, fatigue, sleep and dysautonomia core clusters beyond chance after adjustment for verbosity. When the symptoms began could not be established, and the data cannot attribute the pattern to vaccination rather than to intercurrent illness or to selection into long-term response. The classifier did not meet its pre-registered accuracy standard, though a blind single-coder audit of 306 disagreements found it right in 86% of its unmatched positives against 60% for CDC's; the findings are hypothesis-generating.

Key Points

- The text classifier failed its pre-registered accuracy gate for eight of nine domains, so every result is a sensitivity analysis; within it, 1.01% of 8.6 million person-doses with a long-term v-safe survey described a multi-domain symptom pattern meeting a pre-registered PACVS-like definition.

- Cognitive symptoms, fatigue, sleep disturbance and dysautonomia co-occurred 1.4 to 2.7 times more often than chance under every null tried, including one that fixes each person's own number of symptoms; the aggregate excess did not survive those nulls, the core did.

- The registered onset test could not establish when the symptoms began, and VAERS captured these cases at nearly the same rate as named acute events; the findings are hypothesis-generating and call for independent, multi-coder validation of the classifier before any confirmatory claim.

Introduction

Post-acute COVID-19 vaccination syndrome (PACVS) is a complex multi-system condition following COVID-19 vaccination, with symptoms similar to long COVID: fatigue, cognitive difficulty ("brain fog") and post-exertional malaise among them. Assigning causality to vaccination has been difficult, especially for a complex multi-system disorder [1, 2]. That leaves patients without the coherent clinical entity on which compensation, treatment and research depend [3].

Prevalence estimates range from 0.003% to 0.9% of COVID-19 vaccine recipients [2]. The higher figure comes from an occupational survey of workers; the lower relies on verified reports, which understate the rate. Case series have described a coherent presentation [4–15], but their patients came forward attributing symptoms to vaccination. Screening and pharmacovigilance do not identify PACVS, because there is no disease entity to code to: a system can flag fatigue or brain fog one symptom at a time, but not a constellation.

A syndrome that spans several organ systems and begins weeks to months after vaccination is hard for routine safety surveillance to see. Spontaneous reports are coded one symptom at a time to MedDRA Preferred Terms, so a multi-system pattern is split across many terms. Their denominators are also unknown, so changes in reporting can reflect awareness of the system or the salience of the vaccination campaign rather than the rate of events [16]. Active surveillance designs usually count events inside a fixed risk window after the dose, so later problems fall outside it.

v-safe, CDC's smartphone check-in system, followed about 10 million COVID-19 vaccine recipients in the United States for up to a year, soliciting their condition at fixed time points, and has been used to examine preliminary signals from VAERS [17]. A followed cohort with solicited reports gives the denominator that stimulated reporting lacks. v-safe asked open questions at 3, 6 and 12 months after a dose. Released under FOIA, these answers are the largest body of long-term, participant-written text on post-vaccination health. Levi and Lu [22] analysed v-safe's structured check-in answers: among 9,904,924 enrollees aged 16 or older, 185,285 (1.87%) reported worse health lasting at least three months that they attributed to vaccination (2.24% of women), while 1.92% reported prolonged worse health without attributing it. Their phenotype rests on the participant's attribution and the structured health answers. The present study differs in using the free text to define a symptom-based phenotype that does not depend on attribution, in testing whether symptom domains cluster against a null model, and in examining onset and recorded VAERS filing. The two analyses were independent; the preprint was read on 12 November 2026, after this study was registered and after its registered results had been computed.

We asked four pre-registered questions:

1.  Coherence (Q1). Do nine candidate PACVS symptom domains co-occur within people more than their individual frequencies predict?

2.  Natural history (Q2). Do cases' symptoms begin within six weeks of a dose, and do they persist more than in people who had a severe first week and recovered?

3.  Detection (Q3). Are cases less likely than people with named acute diagnoses to reach VAERS, and are their VAERS reports coded to a wider spread of terms?

4.  Burden (Q4). How often do cases report health impact, medical care and call-centre contact? (Descriptive.)

Each entry was linked to its registrant, the check-in it answered, the most recent preceding dose, CDC's MedDRA codes and call-centre outcomes within 90 days. Entries redacted under FOIA exemption (b)[6] were excluded. OpenVSafe match data were read from its public per-registrant pages [18] on 2026-11-10 and 2026-11-11 for the 44,954 people whom the call-centre data marked as VAERS-filed, at one request per second, within the access the site's robots.txt allows for those pages; no bulk file exists, and no permission beyond the site's published access rules was sought. Only the VAERS ID, match score, confidence and runner-up score were kept.

Pre-registration

The protocol was registered on OSF (osf.io/gnfpd) on 2026-11-11, before any 3-, 6- or 12-month text was classified or read. Code enforced this: long-term text could not be classified until the registration link was set. The taxonomy (v1.0) was frozen on 2026-11-10. The closest prior work, Levi and Lu's analysis of v-safe's structured answers [22], was identified on 12 November 2026, after registration and after the registered analyses had been run; it did not change the registered plan, and no amendment was filed for it. Two amendments were filed before the results they govern were computed:

- Amendment 1 replaced the planned two-coder gold set, for which no coders were available, with CDC's MedDRA coding as the reference standard.

- Amendment 2 replaced the registered language model (GLM-5.3) with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, because the first model's quota cost made the full run infeasible.

Classification

Layer 1 is a keyword lexicon for 21 themes, including the nine PACVS domains: fatigue and post-exertional malaise, cognitive, dysautonomia, sensory-neuropathic, sleep, pain, gastrointestinal, menstrual and cardiorespiratory. It also tags three exclusion themes (named acute diagnosis, unrelated cause, upper-respiratory infection) and flags entries about the time before vaccination. It was run on all entries.

Layer 3 is a language model (deepseek/deepseek-v4.1-flash, reasoning off, prompt v0.1) given the same closed theme list. It labelled every unique long-term text to which layer 1 gave no theme: 649,516 entries, 386,862 unique texts. Requests went through a zero-data-retention route; texts containing a detectable e-mail address or phone number were not sent. The final classifier is the union of the two layers.

Validation and the confirmatory gate

An entry was positive for a domain in the reference if CDC coded it to at least one Preferred Term listed for that domain in the frozen map. All long-term entries with a CDC code were evaluated, excluding redacted and pre-vaccine entries. We computed precision and recall with 95% Wilson intervals. A domain was confirmatory if both were at least 0.85 for the final classifier. As registered, if any domain failed, Q1 to Q3 were to be run on passing domains (primary) and on all nine (sensitivity).

Case definition and comparison groups

The unit is a person-dose. A case meets three criteria:

1.  At a 3-, 6- or 12-month survey S, the person reported health worse than before vaccination, or a health impact (unable to do normal activities, missed work or school, or needed care).

2.  Free text at S names at least two PACVS domains, or free text across at least two long-term surveys names at least two between them.

3.  Free text at S names no exclusion theme.

Three comparison groups were defined, mutually exclusive, with priority case, then (b), (c), (a):

- (a) No symptoms: no long-term survey worse or with impact, and no PACVS text.

- (b) Acute event: free text at any survey of that dose names an acute diagnosis.

- (c) Severe week 1, recovered: an impact or at least two domains in week 1, then no long-term survey worse or with impact.

Statistical analysis

Q1. We built a person-dose by domain matrix for person-doses with long-term text. The statistic is the sum, over the 36 domain pairs, of the people having both. The null permutes each domain column independently 1,000 times (seed 20261110), keeping each domain's frequency. The one-sided p-value is (1 + number of null values at least the observed) / 1,001. Per-pair p-values were Holm-adjusted. The test was repeated within each dose and manufacturer with at least 1,000 person-doses, and on VAERS COVID-19 report text classified by layer 1.

Q2. Onset is the first survey type (week 1, six weeks, 3, 6 or 12 months) whose text names any of the case's long-term domains. Each case's null probability of early onset is the share of their completed check-ins for that dose that were in week 1 or the six-week period. The number of early-onset cases was compared with this Poisson-binomial null by an exact one-sided test. Persistence compares cases with group (c) on the share naming a PACVS domain at a later long-term survey, among those who completed one. We used a risk difference with a Newcombe interval and a one-sided z-test.

Q3. Analysis was at the person level: a case if any dose was a case, otherwise group (b) if any dose was. A person reached VAERS if a call-centre contact within 90 days of an entry recorded a VAERS filing, or OpenVSafe [18], a project of OpenVAERS that matches v-safe records to VAERS reports on timing, dose and shared symptoms, matched them to a report. We call this measure recorded VAERS filing. It is not VAERS capture in general: a report filed without call-centre contact and not matched by OpenVSafe is not counted. Matches needed a score of at least 8 of 11, and reports matched to more than one person were dropped (main set: 25,913 people). A sensitivity set used high-confidence matches only (23,925 people). Capture was compared as a rate ratio with a log-scale Wald interval. Dispersion compared the Shannon entropy of matched reports' MedDRA terms between groups, using a 1,000-fold permutation of group labels.

Q4 and attrition. Q4 gives rates per 1,000 for health impact, care and call-centre cases by group. Case prevalence was bounded by assuming every person-dose without a long-term survey was well (lower bound) or a case (upper bound).

Disclosure. Only aggregates were exported. Any cell with fewer than 10 people was suppressed, and nothing was reported below the national level. No free text was quoted.

Decisions where the registration was silent

- Survey types 'day 0' to 'day 7' count as week 1 and 'week 2' to 'week 6' as six weeks; impact is any health-impact answer other than 'N/A' or blank; entries flagged as pre-vaccine by either layer were dropped.

- The registered layer 2 (embedding classifier) was not built; the final classifier is layers 1 and 3. Layer 3 labelled long-term text only, so week-1 and six-week text was classified by layer 1 alone.

- Persistence uses each case's first worse-or-impact long-term survey, and group (c)'s first long-term survey, as the reference point; Q3 is run per person, because VAERS outcomes attach to people.

- Q1's permutation was re-implemented after registration for speed (each domain's positive rows drawn directly), which gives the same null distribution.

- The model-sensitivity check used 2,000 long-term residue entries (seed 1), the pilot's sample size, because the registration fixed none.

Model-sensitivity check

As registered, a second model, claude-opus-5-5 through the Anthropic API, labelled a fixed sample of long-term text: 2,000 long-term residue entries drawn with seed 1 (the pilot's sample size, since the registration fixed none), 1,687 unique texts. A theme is model-sensitive if its entry count differs by more than 20% between the two models on the texts both labelled.

Analyses not in the pre-registration

These were specified after the registered results were seen, each for the reason given, and are reported as exploratory. They do not replace any registered result.

1.  Text-matched co-occurrence null. Reason: the registered null ignores how much each person-dose wrote. Each domain's positives are placed at random within strata of long-term characters (deciles) and of entries (1, 2, 3, 4 or more) crossed with deciles; expected pair counts are the within-stratum products summed.

2.  Curveball null. Reason: the text-matched null still lets a person-dose's own domain count vary. Curveball trades keep every person-dose's domain count and every domain's frequency (30 burn-in rounds, 3 between samples, 1,000 samples, seed 20261110), unstratified and within entries (1, 2, 3 or more) and text-length tertiles. The total pair count is then fixed by construction, so only the 36 pairs are tested, with upper- and lower-tail p-values.

3.  Exclusion criterion variants. Reason: the exclusion tags had low precision. Criterion 3 and group (b) from keyword tags only, and criterion 3 dropped; group sizes, Q2 and Q3 for each.

4.  Persistence by qualification route and against group (d). Reason: the across-surveys route is partly circular and group (c) has no long-term impact by definition. Cases that met criterion 2 at a single survey are separated; group (d) is long-term responders worse or impacted at a long-term survey who named fewer than two domains there, excluding cases and acute events.

5.  Onset from structured week-1 answers. Reason: free text under-records early symptoms, check-boxes do not. Week-1 symptoms and severity for fatigue (fatigue field or 'Fatigue or tiredness'), pain (headache, joint pains, muscle or body aches) and gastrointestinal (nausea, diarrhoea, vomiting, abdominal pain), among cases with the domain and in group (a).

6.  Symmetric classifier. Reason: layer 3 had labelled long-term text only. It was also run on cases' early residue (38,647 entries) and the onset test repeated, with a second null uniform over completed survey types.

7.  Validation audit. Reason: CDC coding is not ground truth. 306 long-term entries where layer 3 and CDC disagree on a domain (17 per domain per side), seed 20261112, drawn for blind coding by the author.

8.  Dispersion with small-sample corrections. Reason: entropy rises with code count. Miller-Madow correction, rarefaction to equal code counts, two-sided permutation test.

9.  Redacted entries included in the Q1 rows, to bound the (b)[6] redactions; and the case count without criterion 3.

10. Levi and Lu's cohorts rebuilt from the structured answers (their rules; no age field exists, so the adult check-in file stands in for 16 or older), as a linkage check, and cross-tabulated with the text-defined case at person level with Cohen's kappa.

11. Q1 within each sex. Reason: a reviewer asked whether the cluster holds in women and men separately, since 77.5% of cases are women. The registered, text-matched (entries × character deciles) and curveball nulls were run within women and within men, and the registered null within sex × dose strata; 'Other' and 'Unknown' sex were not tested.

12. Core-subgraph statistic. Reason: a sum over all 36 pairs hides where the excess sits. Observed and expected co-occurrences were summed over the six pairs among cognitive symptoms, fatigue, sleep and dysautonomia, and over the other 30 pairs, under each null, from the saved pair tables; no test is attached to the sums.

Results

Corpus

The twelve updated FOIA releases and the under-3 file hold 7,819,275 rows, 7,819,251 after de-duplication; the release description gives 7,554,929 entries, and the difference is unexplained. Of the 7,819,251 entries, 1,595,121 came from long-term surveys, written by 1,207,426 people: 565,626 at 3 months, 586,468 at 6 months and 443,027 at 12 months. A further 5,791,000 came from week-1 and six-week surveys, and 433,130 could not be matched to a survey. The 144,972,101 check-ins are the 144,856,043 rows of the adult file and 116,294 of the under-3 file in our download, de-duplicated by response ID; Levi and Lu report 151,581,805 adult rows, so the file versions differ. Layer 3 labelled all 386,862 unique long-term residue texts with no failed requests after retry. The provider's per-request charges logged by the run total US$0.022 for that run and US$0.0005 for the later runs; the main run's row in the run table was lost when its process dropped a connection, so its token count is not logged.

Classifier validation: one domain passed

Only fatigue passed the gate (precision 0.961, recall 0.859). Layer 3 raised recall in every domain but lowered precision. Dysautonomia, which passed on keywords alone (0.978 / 0.872), failed on precision once layer 3 was added (0.774). Eight domains are therefore exploratory, and the registered primary analysis, which needs at least two passing domains, could not be run. Every result below is the registered nine-domain sensitivity analysis.

The exclusion themes also had low precision against the reference: unrelated cause 0.144, acute diagnosis 0.311. A post-hoc look at the CDC terms on these entries suggests most of the shortfall comes from gaps in the frozen map rather than from wrong tags. The commonest terms were COVID-19, a positive SARS-CoV-2 test, nasopharyngitis, cerebrovascular accident and thrombosis, none of which are in the map. Two real errors were seen: entries mentioning a negative COVID-19 test were tagged as an unrelated cause, and chronic conditions were tagged as acute diagnoses.

Q1: the domains cluster

Among 1,267,509 person-doses with long-term text, domain pairs co-occurred 235,184 times against 188,776 expected under the null (ratio 1.25, p = 0.001). The test rejected in all seven strata with at least 1,000 person-doses: doses 1 to 4 (ratios 1.21 to 1.39) and Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna and Janssen (1.22 to 1.26). This meets the registered criterion for reproducibility, though at these sample sizes any real dependence reaches p = 0.001, so the ratios carry the information. The null keeps each domain's frequency but not each person's amount of text: a person-dose with more entries or longer entries has more chances to name several domains, and this alone produces some excess co-occurrence.

Twenty-three of the 36 pairs exceeded the null after Holm adjustment (adjusted p = 0.036). The strongest ties were cognitive symptoms with sleep (4.1 times expected), fatigue (3.0) and dysautonomia (2.2). The other 13 pairs were at or below chance:

- all eight pairs involving menstrual symptoms;

- sensory symptoms with fatigue, gastrointestinal or cardiorespiratory symptoms;

- cardiorespiratory with gastrointestinal symptoms;

- dysautonomia with pain.

Replication in VAERS. We applied the keyword classifier to the symptom text of 1,057,077 VAERS reports for COVID-19 vaccines (2020 to 2026), one row per report. Pairs co-occurred 486,503 times against 262,471 expected (ratio 1.85, p = 0.001). Thirty-one of 36 pairs exceeded the null after Holm adjustment. As in v-safe, the strongest tie was cognitive symptoms with sleep (4.2 times expected), followed by cognitive symptoms with dysautonomia (2.9) and fatigue (2.8). Menstrual symptoms again did not cluster with fatigue, pain or sensory symptoms. VAERS reports mostly describe acute reactions, in which several symptoms often appear together, so this replication shows the same structure but not its timing.

Q2: onset test uninformative, strong persistence

Onset. Of 87,217 cases with an onset survey, 24,316 (27.9%) first described their case domains in week 1 or by six weeks. 62,192 (71.3%) were expected under the null, so H0 is not rejected (p = 1.0). 72% first described them at 3 months or later; 3 months was the commonest first survey (34%).

Model sensitivity (registered check)

On the fixed sample of 2,000 long-term residue entries (1,687 unique texts, all labelled by both models, no failures or refusals), four of nine PACVS domains were model-sensitive by the registered 20% rule: fatigue, menstrual, cardiorespiratory and cognitive symptoms (Table 2). Sleep had too few entries to report. Dysautonomia, gastrointestinal, pain and sensory-neuropathic symptoms were stable. Among the other themes, unrelated cause, persistence and care sought were flagged; Claude Opus applied each more often than DeepSeek. The sample is residue text, where the keyword layer found nothing, so the counts per domain are small and the shift statistic is noisy below about 50 entries. The direction for fatigue is nonetheless clear: on such text DeepSeek labelled it less than half as often as Claude. An earlier pilot on 2,000 random early entries had found every PACVS domain within 13% between the two models; the disagreement is specific to the harder long-term residue.

Discussion

The pre-registered confirmatory analysis could not be run, because only one of nine domains met the accuracy gate. In the registered sensitivity analysis, three of five hypotheses were supported:

- symptoms cluster (Q1);

- they persist (Q2);

- cases reach VAERS less often than people with named acute diagnoses (Q3).

Two were not supported, and both results ran in the opposite direction: early onset (Q2) and dispersed VAERS coding (Q3). The supported effects differ greatly in size. Clustering and persistence are large; under-capture is a 6% difference between two rates near 5%.

[]

Figure 4. analysis_sens9 Q1–Q3 aggregates · registered tests, nine-domain sensitivity analysis

Clustering. The excess co-occurrence centres on cognitive symptoms, fatigue, sleep and dysautonomia. That is the core described for PACVS and for long COVID. It held in every dose and manufacturer stratum, and in women and in men. Pain and gastrointestinal symptoms, the commonest domains, contributed large absolute counts but smaller excesses. Menstrual and sensory symptoms did not join the cluster. The text-matched null (Table 3, Figure 5) settled most of it: matching on amount of text removed most of the aggregate excess, from 1.25 to 1.04, but the cognitive, fatigue, sleep and dysautonomia core remained at 1.6 to 2.5 times chance, and 16 pairs stayed above the null. A curveball null that fixes each person-dose's domain count as well (Table 11) gives the same core at 1.4 to 2.7 times, with 13 to 15 pairs above it; under that null the aggregate ratio is 1 by construction. Summed over the six pairs among the four core domains (Table 13), co-occurrence was 1.58 times expected under the registered null, 1.30 to 1.32 under the text-matched nulls and 1.21 to 1.22 under the curveball null, against 1.22, 1.02 to 1.04 and 0.98 for the other 30 pairs: the whole excess that survives the strict nulls sits in that core. Not every core pair carries it; fatigue with dysautonomia lay at or below chance under every null, and the strongest ties all involve cognitive symptoms, the domain with the lowest recall (0.74) among the four. The syndrome-like finding is that core, not the aggregate ratio.

Onset. The registered hypothesis assumed that a vaccine-related syndrome would be described first in the weeks after the dose. Instead, 72% of cases first described their case domains at 3 months or later. This has three readings, and our data cannot separate them:

- Late-onset or late-recognised effects. If real, they would be missed by any design that counts only events in a fixed window after the dose, often 1 to 42 days.

- Background illness. Late onset is also what intercurrent illness, including COVID-19 infection, would produce. It weakens the case for attribution to the dose.

- Late reporting. Symptoms present early may be written down only once they have lasted, or once an open-ended long-term survey invites a description that the check-box surveys of the first weeks did not.

Three features of the data bias our onset measure towards late description:

1.  Early surveys collected symptoms mainly through checkboxes, while long-term surveys were open-ended.

2.  Early text was classified by keywords only, which had lower recall.

3.  Daily week-1 check-ins inflate the expected early share to 71%.

Both re-examinations were run (Table 5). Classifying early and late text alike raised the early share from 27.9% to 32.7%, still below even a null spread evenly over survey types. Structured week-1 answers show that most cases had ticked fatigue or pain in week 1, but so had most people who were severe and recovered and a third of those who stayed well: the checkboxes record reactogenicity, which cannot be told apart from the start of a persistent syndrome. The registered test therefore records when symptoms were first written down, not when they began. It is uninformative about onset, and the structured data cannot settle it either.

Persistence. Persistence in 60% of cases, against 1.5% of group (c), is large. Part of the gap is built into the definitions: group (c) by construction reported no worse health or impact at long-term surveys. Cases, in turn, were selected for naming at least two domains at a long-term survey, which makes naming one at a later survey more likely. Two checks specified after the results were seen, and so not pre-registered, narrow the gap without closing it (Table 7): restricting to cases that met criterion 2 at a single survey lowers persistence to 47.1%, and group (d), people with the same worse health or impact who named one domain or none, has 10.5% persistence, so the strictest difference is 36.6 points rather than 58.9. Group (d) is the comparison the question needs, since it measures how often a single long-term complaint recurs in the same responders. The comparison shows that the multi-domain pattern is not a slow resolution of a severe first week and recurs more often than other long-term complaints among people who kept answering, and little more. It does not show how persistence compares with the general population or with the people who stopped responding, and the risk ratio in Figure 4 should not be read as an effect size.

Detection. Cases were about 6% less likely than people with named acute diagnoses to reach VAERS, a small but clear difference. Their VAERS reports were, contrary to our hypothesis, coded to a narrower spread of terms, a difference that held under the Miller-Madow correction, rarefaction and a two-sided test. VAERS coding does not appear to scatter this pattern. One possible reason is that the few cases who reach VAERS are those with a more recognisable presentation. On these data, the detection problem, if there is one, lies in the 95% of cases who never reach VAERS at all, which this comparison cannot see, rather than in a lower filing rate than acute events or in coding. Under the two alternative readings of the exclusion criterion (Table 4) the rate ratio fell to 0.82 and 0.78, because the language model's acute-diagnosis tags had diluted group (b) with chronic conditions; the registered figure is the most conservative of the three.

Model sensitivity. The two language models agreed on early text but not on the long-term residue, the text the keywords had missed. Fatigue, the only domain to pass the gate, was labelled less than half as often there by DeepSeek as by Claude Opus. The final classifier's fatigue recall of 0.86, and so its passing the gate, depends on which model labelled the residue. Cardiorespiratory, cognitive and menstrual symptoms were also model-sensitive, and the unrelated-cause exclusion was applied 45% more often by Claude, so the case count itself would differ between models.

Relation to earlier work. Case series of PACVS describe fatigue, cognitive impairment, post-exertional malaise, dysautonomia and neuropathic symptoms as the core presentation [4–6, 10, 12], and a shared clinical and immunological profile with post-acute COVID-19 has been reported [4]. The cluster found here, centred on cognitive symptoms, fatigue, sleep and dysautonomia, matches that core. Sensory-neuropathic symptoms, prominent in some series [8, 9, 12], did not join it, which may reflect the classifier's low recall for that domain (0.25). Published prevalence estimates run from 0.003% to 0.9% [2]; the 1.01% of person-doses with a long-term survey here sits at the top of that range, before any adjustment for attrition or classifier error, and is a share of responses rather than of recipients. Levi and Lu's analysis of v-safe's structured answers found 1.87% of participants reporting worse health lasting at least three months that they attributed to vaccination [22]; our 1.01% applies a stricter definition, at least two symptom domains in free text and no named alternative cause, to long-term responders. Rebuilding their cohorts from the structured answers reproduced their dose-1 and dose-2 counts within 0.5% (Table 10), and the two definitions overlap only partly: a third of text-defined cases attributed worse health to vaccination on a check-box, and a sixth of their cohort met the text definition (kappa 0.22). The text-defined phenotype is therefore not a relabelling of self-attribution. The predominance of women among cases (77.5%) matches case series [5, 6], but v-safe registrants were also mostly women, and the no-symptoms group was 58% women.

Limitations

- The classifier failed its gate. Eight of nine domains fell short of 0.85 precision or recall, so every analysis rests on exploratory labels. Low recall undercounts cases; low precision in some domains overcounts them. Recall was 0.74 for cognitive symptoms and 0.72 for sleep, the two domains that drive the core cluster, so the domains most responsible for the signal are among the least completely counted. Independent coding of a sample sized for domain-level precision and recall with usable confidence intervals is required before any confirmatory claim.

- The co-occurrence null ignores amount of text. Each permutation keeps a domain's frequency but gives every person-dose the same chance of each domain, whatever they wrote. More or longer entries give more chances to name several domains. The text-matched and curveball nulls removed the aggregate excess and left a specific core; the registered ratio of 1.25 should not be quoted without them.

- The reference is coding, not truth. CDC coded entries for adverse-event reporting, often to the most prominent symptom. The frozen map was built from the lexicon's own terms and misses relevant Preferred Terms, as the exclusion themes showed. Measured precision is likely too low for some domains, but we did not alter the map after registration; the blind audit (Table 12) supports this reading but comes from a single coder, the author, blind to side but not to the hypotheses, so it cannot rescue the registered gate.

- Unequal classification over time. Layer 3 labelled only long-term text, which favours finding domains at long-term surveys. This affects onset (Q2) most.

- The one gate pass is model-dependent. On the registered model-sensitivity sample, four PACVS domains, including fatigue, were model-sensitive, so fatigue's passing the gate is specific to the model that labelled the residue.

- Self-described text, not diagnosis. A case describes a pattern in their own words. No clinical confirmation was available.

- No age and no infection history. The linked release carries no age field, so no result is age-adjusted and Levi and Lu's age restriction could not be applied. Intercurrent COVID-19 infection and other medical events are visible only through the exclusion tags, so a late vaccine effect cannot be told from background illness.

- Attrition. Only 49% of person-doses with a check-in completed a long-term survey, so the prevalence bounds are wide. People with a week-1 health impact were more likely to respond (60.1% against 42.4%), which may inflate the observed 1.01%, and v-safe registrants were self-selected.

- Exclusions are imperfect. The unrelated-cause and acute-diagnosis tags had precision of 0.14 and 0.31 against CDC coding, and negative COVID-19 tests and chronic conditions were sometimes tagged as exclusions. Criterion 3 and group (b) are therefore leaky: some true cases are removed, and the VAERS rate ratio moves from 0.94 to 0.78 under other readings of the criterion.

- Comparison groups are partly defined by the outcome. Group (c)'s low persistence and cases' high persistence are both partly built into their definitions; the strictest contrast, same-survey cases against group (d), was specified after the results were seen.

- VAERS linkage and the detection test. VAERS capture relies on call-centre dispositions and on OpenVSafe's probabilistic matches [18], which we did not validate independently. Matched reports exist only for people the call centre recorded as filing. Q3 was run at person level, while the groups are defined per dose. A contrast between two filing rates near 5% is a weak test of under-capture and says nothing about the cases who never reach VAERS.

- Implementation choices after registration. These are listed in Methods; all were made without seeing results.

Conclusions

1.01% of person-doses with a long-term v-safe survey describe a multi-domain symptom pattern. Its domains cluster beyond chance; once the null is matched on how much people wrote and on each person's own number of symptoms, the clustering is confined to a core of cognitive symptoms, fatigue, sleep and dysautonomia, in women and in men alike. It persists across surveys and reaches VAERS slightly less often than named acute events. It was mostly first described months after vaccination, which leaves open whether it reflects late vaccine effects, background illness including COVID-19 infection, or late reporting; these data do not establish that vaccination caused the pattern, its prevalence among vaccine recipients, or its status as a clinical entity. Because the classifier did not meet its pre-registered accuracy standard, these findings are hypothesis-generating. The next steps are independent multi-coder validation of the classifier on a sample sized for domain-level accuracy, an onset analysis that treats the survey schedule as the observation process, and replication in other free-text or survey sources.
